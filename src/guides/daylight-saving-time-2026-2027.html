Daylight saving time (DST) moves clocks forward by an hour in spring and back in autumn. Only some countries use it, and those that do change on different dates. The result is that the time difference between two cities can change several times a year.

The dates below come from the IANA time zone database, the same data your phone and computer use. Times are local.

In the southern hemisphere, summer falls at the end of the calendar year, so DST ends in April and starts again in September or October.

The weeks when the gap between the US and Europe changes

The United States moves its clocks about three weeks before Europe in spring, and one week after Europe in autumn. During those weeks the usual gap between New York and London shrinks by an hour.

{{GAP_TABLE}}

If you have a recurring meeting between North America and Europe, expect it to move by an hour on one side during these weeks, depending on which city's time the invite uses.

When one side of a meeting uses DST and the other does not, the time difference changes twice a year. Seoul and London, for example, are 8 hours apart in summer and 9 hours apart in winter.

How to avoid mistakes

  • Create recurring invites in a calendar app, not as a fixed time written in an email. Calendar apps convert each occurrence using the right rules.
  • Say which city's time a recurring meeting follows. “Every Monday at 09:00 New York time” is clear. “Every Monday at 14:00 UTC” moves for everyone who uses DST.
  • Check any date that falls within a week of a clock change in the planner. It uses your browser's time zone data, so the offsets shown are the ones that will apply on that date.
