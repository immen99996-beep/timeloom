Most scheduling mistakes across countries come from four things: people compare clocks instead of working hours, forget that the other side may already be on a different date, miss a public holiday, or schedule during the few weeks when one country has changed its clocks and the other has not. This guide goes through each one with real numbers.

Compare working hours, not clocks

Knowing that Seoul is 8 hours ahead of London in summer does not tell you when to meet. What matters is where the two working days overlap. If both teams work 09:00 to 18:00, the overlap is the part of the Seoul day that falls after 09:00 in London.

Two things stand out. First, several popular pairs, such as Seoul and New York, have no overlap at all inside normal hours. Someone has to join early or stay late. Second, the overlap is not the same all year. Seoul and London share one hour in July and none in January, because the UK moves its clocks and Korea does not.

Check the date on the other side

A Tuesday 09:00 call from Seoul is Monday evening in New York. This is easy to handle when you know it, and easy to get wrong in a calendar invite that only shows one date. When the other side is on a different date, write both dates in the invite, for example “Tue 09:00 KST / Mon 20:00 EDT”.

The date also decides which holidays apply. A meeting at 08:00 on 1 January in Sydney is still 31 December in London, and 31 December is a normal working day there.

Check public holidays on every side

Holidays that one team takes for granted are often invisible to the other. A few that regularly catch international teams out:

  • Lunar New Year closes offices for several days in China, South Korea, Vietnam, Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia. The dates move every year.
  • Chuseok in South Korea is a three-day holiday in September or early October.
  • Golden Week in Japan runs from late April into early May, and China has its own National Day week starting on 1 October.
  • Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha are public holidays across much of the Middle East, North Africa and Southeast Asia. Their exact dates depend on the moon and are sometimes confirmed only a few days ahead.
  • Thanksgiving in the United States falls on the fourth Thursday of November, and many people also take the Friday off.

Watch the weeks when clocks change

The United States and Europe change their clocks on different dates. For a few weeks each spring and autumn, New York is 4 hours behind London instead of 5. A weekly call that is fixed in one city's time will move by an hour for the other city during those weeks. The daylight saving time guide lists the exact dates for 2026 and 2027.

Share the inconvenient slot

When there is no overlap, one group ends up joining outside its working day. For a recurring meeting, rotate that cost: alternate between an early slot for one side and a late slot for the other. It is fairer, and people are more likely to turn up.

A short checklist

  1. List every location, not just every country. The United States, Canada, Australia, Brazil, Mexico, Russia and Indonesia each have more than one time zone.
  2. Find the overlap of working hours, then pick a slot inside it.
  3. Check the local date for each person.
  4. Check weekends and public holidays for each country on that local date.
  5. If the meeting repeats, check the weeks when clocks change.
  6. Write the time in every relevant zone in the invite.
